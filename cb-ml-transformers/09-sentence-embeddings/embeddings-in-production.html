Embeddings in Production

Module epilogue, and the ops manual no model card ever wrote. encode() was the easy half — you now own two million vectors, and they have opinions about RAM, recall, and release management. This page covers the vector layer's operational life: what precision your floats actually need, the knobs inside your ANN index (and which one silently trades away recall), the versioning law that turns embedder upgrades into schema migrations, and the end-to-end cost math for a corpus at scale. M12.1 covers the RAG pipeline above this layer — chunking, hybrid search, HNSW's introduction; this page lives below it, where the bytes are.

The storage bill: precision and dimensions

A vector database's core bill is one multiplication — n vectors × d dimensions × bytes per number — and both of the levers you'd expect from M11.3 apply to vectors too. fp32 (the default): 1.536 KB per MiniLM vector. fp16: half that, recall loss unmeasurable. int8 scalar quantization: quarter, typically ~1% recall cost. Binary quantization — one bit per dimension, sign only — is the aggressive endpoint: 48 bytes per vector, 32× smaller than fp32, at a 5–10% recall hit… which a rescoring trick largely recovers: search coarsely with binary vectors (Hamming distance — hardware-native and absurdly fast), then re-rank the top few hundred candidates with their float versions kept on disk. Sound familiar? It's M12.2's cheap-wide-net/expensive-fine-net, applied to storage. The second lever is newer and worth knowing by name: Matryoshka embeddings — models trained so that meaning concentrates in the leading dimensions, letting you truncate a 768-d vector to its first 256 dims and keep most retrieval quality. Dimensionality stops being fixed at training time and becomes a serving decision.

index bytes ≈ n · d · bytes-per-dim  (+ graph overhead ≈ 1.5×)
In plain words: vectors × dimensions × precision, plus roughly half again for the ANN graph's link structure. Every optimization on this page attacks one of the three factors — fewer bytes (quantize), fewer dims (truncate), or smarter neighbors (the index itself).

ANN under the hood: HNSW's bill and its one big dial

M12.1 introduces HNSW's skip-list-of-graphs idea; here's the part you'll actually operate. The index has two build-time knobs — M (links per node: more = better recall ceiling, more RAM — the ~1.5× overhead in the formula above) and efConstruction (build-time search width: higher = better graph, slower indexing) — and one query-time dial that matters more than both: efSearch, how many candidate nodes each query explores. It is a direct recall↔latency dial: ef=10 might give recall 0.82 at 0.4 ms; ef=200, recall 0.99 at 2.8 ms. Two operational truths follow. First, your vector DB's default is a marketing number — tuned to look fast; measure recall at your ef against brute force on your data (the M9.5 harness does this in ten lines) before trusting it. Second, deletes are lies: HNSW graphs can't cheaply unlink nodes, so deletions are usually tombstones — marked dead, still occupying graph and RAM — and a corpus with heavy churn needs periodic index rebuilds or its performance quietly rots.

The versioning law: one index, one model

M1.1 taught it as geometry, M9.5 flagged it as a regression; here it becomes operations. Vectors are only comparable within the geometry that made them — so the embedder version is effectively your index's schema version, and changing it (upgrade, fine-tune, even a "minor" revision) is a migration, never a patch. The production pattern is blue-green re-indexing: keep v1 serving; build the v2 index in a shadow pipeline (re-embed everything — budget the throughput with M7.1's batching); run a dual-read window where a sample of live queries hits both indexes and you compare recall on the golden set; then flip traffic atomically, keeping v1 warm for rollback. Tag every stored row with its embedder version so a mixed-geometry index is detectable rather than a silent quality collapse. Teams that skip this discover it as a postmortem titled "search got mysteriously worse after the model upgrade."

Freshness, drift, and the monitoring loop

Day-two operations, quickly. Incremental upserts are safe — new documents embedded with the same model version slot into the graph happily (mind the tombstone caveat for the documents they replace). The slow threat is content drift: your corpus's vocabulary and topics evolve — new products, new jargon, new question styles — while the embedder's geometry stands still, so retrieval quality erodes without any code change. The defense is the M9.5 harness on a schedule: re-run the golden set weekly, refresh it quarterly with current real queries, and alert on recall deltas like any other SLO. When the metric sags, you know the ladder: threshold/label fixes first, a fresh fine-tune (and this page's migration) when the drift is real.

The cost math, end to end

Close the module with a napkin worthy of M7.2. Ten million chunks: embedding time — at ~2,000 sentences/sec batched on one modest GPU (M7.1), ≈ 1.4 GPU-hours: dollars, not days — re-indexing is cheaper than teams fear. RAM — fp32: 15 GB of vectors + ~50% HNSW overhead ≈ 23 GB (a real machine); int8: ~6 GB total (a small one); binary + rescoring: under 2 GB (a laptop). Queries — HNSW at these scales returns in single-digit milliseconds; your latency budget lives almost entirely in the LLM call downstream (M7.5). The strategic reading: the vector layer's costs are front-loaded (embedding, RAM) and its marginal query is nearly free — the opposite shape from the LLM layer — which is why over-retrieving then reranking (M12.2) is economically sane.

Real numbers

One 384-d fp32 vector = 1.536 KB; 1M ≈ 1.5 GB; 10M ≈ 15 GB — ≈ 23 GB with HNSW overhead, or ~6 GB at int8. Binary + float rescoring: ~0.5 GB + cold floats, recovering most recall at 1/32 the hot storage. efSearch calibration points (typical 1M-vector index): ef 10 → recall ~0.82 @ 0.4 ms · ef 50 → ~0.95 @ 1.1 ms · ef 200 → ~0.99 @ 2.8 ms. Re-embedding 10M chunks with MiniLM: ≈ 1.4 GPU-hours. The bill that surprises teams is never compute — it's RAM held 24/7.

Build: measure YOUR recall-vs-efSearch curve
# pip install faiss-cpu sentence-transformers numpy
import faiss, numpy as np, time
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
docs = [f"Document about topic {i%1000}, variant {i}" for i in range(100_000)]
X = model.encode(docs, batch_size=256, normalize_embeddings=True,
                 show_progress_bar=True).astype("float32")
Q = X[:200] + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.02, (200, 384)).astype("float32")

flat = faiss.IndexFlatIP(384); flat.add(X)          # brute force = ground truth
_, truth = flat.search(Q, 10)

hnsw = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(384, 32)                  # M=32 links/node
hnsw.hnsw.efConstruction = 200
hnsw.add(X)

for ef in [10, 25, 50, 100, 200]:
    hnsw.hnsw.efSearch = ef
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); _, got = hnsw.search(Q, 10)
    ms = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / len(Q) * 1000
    recall = np.mean([len(set(g) & set(t)) / 10 for g, t in zip(got, truth)])
    print(f"efSearch {ef:4d}: recall@10 {recall:.3f} | {ms:.2f} ms/query")

The printed table is this page's second animation, measured on your hardware. Find the ef where recall crosses your requirement, note the latency, and set it deliberately — then run the same sweep against your production DB's equivalent knob and compare it to the default it shipped with.

If you remember one thing

The vector layer has three dials — precision, dimensions, and efSearch — trading RAM and latency against recall you must measure, never assume. And one law: an embedder version is a schema version — new geometry means full re-embed, blue-green swap, version tags. Front-loaded costs, near-free queries: engineer accordingly, and the layer disappears into reliability, which is the highest compliment infrastructure gets.

Interview questions

Your vector DB bill explodes at 50M vectors — what three levers do you pull before switching products?

(1) Precision: fp32 → int8 scalar quantization cuts hot storage 4× for ~1 recall point; binary + float rescoring cuts 32× if recall tolerances allow. (2) Dimensions: a Matryoshka-trained or smaller-d embedder (768→384→256) scales the bill linearly — validated against your golden set, since this is a model change (re-embed + migration). (3) Tiering: most corpora have cold segments — move rarely-hit collections to disk-based indexes (IVF/DiskANN-style) and keep HNSW-in-RAM for the hot set. Then the honest fourth option: check whether n itself is inflated — near-duplicate chunks and dead documents commonly pad 20–30%. Only after these four does "switch products" enter the conversation, because every product obeys the same n·d·bytes formula.

Why can't vectors from two embedder versions share one index?

Similarity is only defined within one learned geometry: each training run places meanings into its own coordinate system (unique up to rotation, shaped by its data and loss), so a v1 document vector and a v2 query vector have no meaningful dot product — scores become noise that still LOOKS like scores. Fine-tuning counts fully: it deliberately moved the geometry (that was the point). Operationally this makes the embedder version a schema version: full re-embed, blue-green index swap, per-row version tags so mixing is detectable. The subtle failure worth naming: a partial migration degrades quietly — no errors, just recall decaying in proportion to the mixed fraction — which is why the flip must be atomic.

How do you determine whether the ANN index (not the embedder) is costing you recall?

Differential measurement: run your golden-set queries against the SAME vectors with brute-force exact search (FAISS IndexFlat) and with the ANN index at production settings. The exact-search recall is the embedder's ceiling; the gap between it and ANN recall is the index's tax. If exact search also misses, the geometry is the problem (fine-tune territory, M9.5); if the gap is material, sweep efSearch/nprobe upward and re-measure — usually a config-line fix. Do this at production filter conditions too: metadata-filtered ANN queries can have much worse recall than unfiltered ones (filtering shrinks the navigable graph), a well-known gotcha this method exposes.

Binary embeddings cut storage 32× — how is retrieval quality preserved?

Two mechanisms. First, high-dimensional grace: keeping only each dimension's sign preserves direction surprisingly well at 384–1024 dims — Hamming distance on bits correlates strongly with cosine on floats, so the top of the ranking survives even though fine ordering blurs. Second, two-stage rescoring: binary search over-retrieves (top 100–500 candidates at negligible cost), then full-precision vectors — kept in cheap cold storage, only fetched for candidates — re-rank to the final top-k. The wide net's recall losses mostly happen BELOW the rescoring cutoff, so end-to-end recall lands within 1–3 points of full-precision at ~1/32 the hot RAM. It's the retrieve-then-rerank pattern (M12.2) implemented inside the storage layer.

When is brute-force (exact) vector search the right choice?

More often than vendors suggest. Under ~100k–1M vectors, exact search is a single matrix multiply (M1.2's grid): a 100k×384 sweep is ~40M multiply-adds — sub-millisecond on GPU, a few ms vectorized on CPU — with recall exactly 1.0, zero build time, zero tuning, trivial deletes and filters. Choose it when: corpora are small-to-medium, correctness is contractual (eval harnesses should ALWAYS use it as ground truth), churn is high (no graph to rot), or you're debugging (removes the ANN variable entirely). Graduate to ANN when p99 latency at your scale demands it — and keep the flat index around forever as the measuring stick the previous question depends on.
