The Encoder Block & Stacking

The block: two moves, two wrappers

Depth as message passing: what layer 2 can see that layer 1 can't

Why repeat the block at all — doesn't one round of attention already let every token see every other token? Yes, and that's exactly why the answer is subtle. Think of each block as one round of message passing: in round 1, every token reads the original versions of its neighbors. "It" can see "cat", but only naked, context-free cat. Meanwhile — in that same round — "cat" has been absorbing "chased", becoming cat-that-chased. Come round 2, when "it" glances at "cat" again, it's reading the updated vector: it receives chased-flavored-cat, a two-step relational fact no single round could deliver. L layers = L rounds = L-hop reasoning chains. That's why "the cat that the dog chased ran away" — where resolving "ran"'s subject requires hopping through a relative clause — needs depth, not just attention.

Six floors of "bank": the layer-by-layer trace

What do the rounds actually accomplish? Probing studies — training tiny diagnostic classifiers on each layer's vectors — show a remarkably consistent progression, and it's worth walking one word up the building. Feed in "she sat by the river bank" and follow "bank": at layer 1 its vector mostly encodes surface identity — wordpiece stitched together, position stamped, faint neighbor awareness. Layers 2–3 resolve part-of-speech and local syntax: bank-the-noun, head of a prepositional phrase. Layers 4–5 do roles and reference: object of "by", co-located with "river", the "sat" event attached. By layer 6, the vector is task-shaped semantics: river-bank, disambiguated, clause integrated — ready for pooling into a sentence embedding. The practical consequence hides in that arc: which floor you tap decides what your representation knows — middle layers often win syntax probes, final layers win semantic tasks, and M9.1's pooling choices are exactly this decision made deliberately.

What stacking does NOT buy

Honesty section, because the dial isn't magic. Depth compounds composition, not quality wholesale — and returns diminish task by task. Sentence similarity saturates early: MiniLM's 6 floors capture nearly everything its 12-floor sibling does (1–3 benchmark points apart), because "what does this sentence mean, roughly" is a shallow-hierarchy question. Multi-step reasoning is the opposite regime — that's where 96-floor models justify themselves, and even they run out of hops (one reason chain-of-thought prompting works, M11.5: it converts missing depth into extra forward passes). Meanwhile every floor costs the same latency, memory, and — per M3.4 — training difficulty that residuals mitigate but don't erase. Depth is a budget you spend on the hierarchy your task actually has.

Connect the dots

You can now do something concrete you couldn't a week ago: open any Hugging Face model page, click "config.json", and know what you're looking at — including spotting why one model is slow (big L, big d), why another truncates your text (max_position_embeddings), and what "L6 vs L12" costs and buys (MiniLM-L12 exists: same width, double depth, ~2× slower, a bit more accurate — a trade you can now reason about instead of guessing).

Build: compute BERT-base's 110M parameters by hand
# every line is arithmetic you can check on paper
V, P, d, L, ffn = 30522, 512, 768, 12, 3072

emb = V*d + P*d + 2*d              # token + position + segment tables
attn_layer = 4*(d*d + d)           # W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O with biases      (5.3-5.4)
ffn_layer  = d*ffn + ffn + ffn*d + d   # expand + compress, with biases  (5.5)
ln_layer   = 2 * 2*d               # two LayerNorms, each γ and β        (5.5)
block      = attn_layer + ffn_layer + ln_layer

total = emb + L*block + 2*d        # + final embedding LayerNorm
print(f"embeddings: {emb/1e6:.2f}M")       # ≈ 23.8M
print(f"per block : {block/1e6:.2f}M")     # ≈ 7.09M
print(f"total     : {total/1e6:.1f}M")     # ≈ 108.9M  ← the famous "110M" ✓

# now YOU: change the constants to MiniLM (30522, 512, 384, 6, 1536)
# and verify ≈22.7M. Then try Llama-3-8B's numbers for sport.

Matching a published parameter count from first principles is a rite of passage — after it, no model size will ever be a mystery number again, and M7.2's memory math becomes trivial (bytes = params × precision).

If you remember one thing

The block = [LayerNorm → attention → +residual] then [LayerNorm → FFN → +residual], shape-preserving, stackable. A transformer is: embeddings + positions → the block × L → output. MiniLM-L6 is literally this block six times at width 384 — a diagram you can now draw, parameter-count, and read from its config file.

Interview questions

Draw/describe a transformer encoder block from memory.

Input: n token vectors of width d. Sublayer 1: LayerNorm, multi-head self-attention, output added to the input (residual). Sublayer 2: LayerNorm, position-wise FFN (d → 4d → GELU → d), again residual-added. Output: n × d, same shape, feeding the next identical block. Attention is the only cross-token operation; the FFN is per-token; the residual stream carries state through the whole stack.

Why must the block preserve its input shape, and what does that enable?

The residual addition x + f(x) requires f(x) to match x's shape, and stacking requires each block's output to be a valid input to the next. This closure makes depth a free hyperparameter — 6 or 96 layers with zero architectural change — and enables tricks that exploit uniformity: layer pruning/early exit, pipeline parallelism across layers (M7.3), and depth-based distillation (M9.4's L6-from-L12 story).

What changes between layer 1 and layer 6 of an encoder, functionally?

Weights are separate per layer (no sharing in standard transformers), and learned function differs systematically: probing shows lower layers capture surface/local patterns, middle layers syntax, upper layers semantics and coreference — each block is another round of gather-then-process, so abstraction compounds. Practical consequences: middle-layer representations often transfer best for similarity tasks, and last-layer ones for classification — and sentence-embedding models choose their pooling layer accordingly (M9.1).

Explain transformer depth as message-passing rounds — what can layer 2 represent that layer 1 cannot?

Each block is one round in which every token updates itself from the current versions of all tokens. In round 1, tokens read only original, context-free vectors. But every token updates simultaneously — so by round 2, a token reads neighbors that already contain round-1 context: "it" attending to "cat" now receives cat-that-chased, a composed two-hop fact ("it refers to the cat which did the chasing") that cannot exist after one round, since it requires reading an updated intermediate. Generally, L layers support L-hop composition chains — why nested syntax and multi-step reference need depth even though single-layer attention is already all-to-all.

Why might you pool sentence embeddings from a middle layer rather than the last?

Layers encode different abstraction levels: probing shows middle layers strongest on syntax and general-purpose lexical semantics, while final layers specialize toward the pretraining objective (e.g., MLM's token-prediction needs), which can make last-layer vectors less transferable for similarity. For some models/tasks, pooling a middle layer — or averaging the first and last layers, a common sentence-transformers recipe — measurably improves retrieval. The operational lesson: "the model's embedding" is really L+1 different embeddings, and which floor you tap is a tunable choice (M9.1), not a given.

Given a config.json, estimate the parameter count.

Embeddings ≈ vocab×d (+ positions×d if learned). Per block: attention 4d² + FFN 2×d×d_ffn (≈ 8d² at the standard 4× ratio) ≈ 12d² total. Total ≈ vocab×d + L×12d². Sanity checks: BERT-base ≈ 23.8M + 12×7.1M ≈ 109M ✓; the formula also shows why doubling d roughly quadruples layer cost (d²) while doubling L only doubles it — a scaling intuition interviewers probe.

MiniLM-L6 vs MiniLM-L12: how do you choose, and what exactly differs?

Same width (384), same heads, same vocab — only depth differs: 6 vs 12 blocks, so ~2× the non-embedding compute and latency for L12, with modest quality gains (≈1–3 points on retrieval benchmarks). Choose by the bottleneck: embedding millions of documents or CPU-serving → L6; scoring quality on hard domains with GPU headroom → L12 or a larger family. The honest engineering answer is "benchmark both on YOUR recall@k" (M9.5's method).
