RNNs & LSTMs, Briefly

RNNs ruled NLP from ~2013 to 2017, and they matter here for exactly two reasons: their failures are the design requirements of the transformer, and "why did attention replace RNNs?" is a top-five interview question. So we go one honest level deep — into the update equation and the LSTM cell itself — because both flaws, and one idea the transformer quietly kept, live inside that machinery.

The idea: read in order, carry a memory

M3's networks took fixed-size inputs. Language is a sequence of arbitrary length — so the recurrent network reads one token at a time, maintaining a single hidden state vector: a running summary of everything read so far. At each step: new state = f(old state, current token) — same weights reused at every position, like one reader moving along the line, updating their mental notes.

Inside one step: the update equation

hₜ = tanh(W_h·hₜ₋₁ + W_x·xₜ + b)
In plain words: one matrix reads the old memory, another reads the new token, their outputs add, and a tanh squashes the result into the new memory. That's the entire recurrent step — an M3.1 neuron layer whose input includes its own previous output.

Look at what's not indexed by position: W_h is the same matrix at every step. That's the elegant part — one reader, any length of text — and the fatal part. Backprop through 100 steps multiplies gradients by (roughly) that same W_h a hundred times, and repeatedly multiplying by a fixed matrix has only two stable outcomes, set by its largest eigenvalue: shrink toward zero (below 1) or blow up (above 1). Sitting exactly at the knife's edge isn't learnable at scale. Exploding gradients got a workable band-aid — gradient clipping, capping the gradient's norm before each update — but note the asymmetry: you can cap a huge number, but no clip can resurrect a gradient that arrived as 10⁻⁸. Vanishing is the unfixable half, and it needed an architectural answer.

Why it breaks: two structural diseases

Disease 1 — long-range decay. You now own the exact mechanism: blame flowing backward through 100 chained multiplications by the same W_h — M3.4's shrinking-bars animation with the layers laid horizontally. Gradients vanish over distance, so the model literally cannot learn dependencies between far-apart words, and the forward pass has the matching problem you just watched: fixed-size memory diluted by every update.

Disease 2 — enforced serialism. h₅ cannot be computed before h₄. Training can't parallelize across the sequence: a 1,000-token document is a 1,000-step chain, no matter how many GPU cores idle. M1.3 taught that hardware loves one giant matrix multiply; RNNs structurally forbid it. This — as much as memory decay — is what killed them: they couldn't scale into the data.

LSTMs: brilliant patch, same wall

The LSTM (1997, dominant 2014–2017) patched disease 1 with gates: small learned valves deciding, at each step, what to write into memory, what to erase, and what to expose — plus a protected "cell state" lane that information can ride with less mangling (a cousin of the residual highways coming in M5.5). GRUs are the simplified two-gate version. The gates genuinely helped: dependencies of 50–100 tokens became learnable, and LSTMs powered Google Translate circa 2016.

Inside the LSTM cell: three gates, one conveyor

The design deserves one concrete look, because it's genuinely clever. Alongside the hidden state, the LSTM adds a second lane: the cell state c, a conveyor belt running straight through time. Three gates — each just a small learned layer ending in a sigmoid, so each outputs numbers between 0 and 1 — act as valves on that conveyor: the forget gate decides how much of the old memory survives, the input gate decides how much of the new candidate gets written, and the output gate decides how much of the memory is exposed as this step's hidden state.

cₜ = f ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + i ⊙ c̃ₜ
In plain words: new memory = (forget gate) × old memory + (input gate) × new candidate. The crucial character is that +: the conveyor is updated additively, not squeezed through a fresh tanh-and-matrix at every step — so a memory the forget gate keeps near 1.0 can ride almost untouched, and gradients can ride back the same way.

But gates only slow the decay — memory is still one fixed-size vector updated serially — and they do nothing at all for disease 2. The field needed random access to the past, computed in parallel. That requirement has a name, and it's the next two pages.

Real numbers

Google's 2016 neural translation system: 8 stacked LSTM layers, trained for about a week on ~96 GPUs — for one bilingual pair. The 2017 transformer paper's base model matched/beat it after 12 hours on 8 GPUs — roughly a 100× drop in training cost, delivered almost entirely by parallelism (disease 2 cured). Quality won headlines; trainability at scale won the war. The decay itself, in one number: with the recurrent matrix's effective gain at a well-behaved 0.9, a gradient crossing 50 steps arrives scaled by 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005 — a 200× attenuation before learning even starts. And the LSTM's last hurrah: ELMo (2018), contextual embeddings from stacked bidirectional LSTMs, briefly state-of-the-art everywhere — then BERT arrived within the year and the recurrent era was over.

Build: watch a gradient vanish (and why clipping can't help)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W = rng.normal(size=(64, 64))
W *= 0.9 / max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(W)))   # set effective gain to 0.9

g = rng.normal(size=64)                     # a gradient arriving at the last step
for steps in [1, 10, 25, 50, 100]:
    gg = g.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        gg = W.T @ gg                       # backprop through one recurrent step
    print(f"after {steps:3d} steps  |grad| = {np.linalg.norm(gg):.2e}")

# now set the scale to 1.1 instead of 0.9 and watch it explode —
# THAT one, clipping can cap. The vanishing one is simply gone.

Five lines reproduce the disease that ended an architecture: distance turns into exponential decay through repeated multiplication by the same matrix. Re-run with gain 1.1 to see the exploding twin — the one gradient clipping actually fixes.

Connect the dots

A fixed-size state that must summarize an unbounded past — you've engineered around this exact shape: a chatbot session where you keep only a rolling summary of the conversation, because you can't resend everything. You know from experience what it forgets: specifics from twenty turns ago. That's an RNN's life at every single token. And the fix you'd instinctively reach for — "just let me look back at the full transcript when needed" — is precisely attention. You've already invented it; the next pages just add matrices.

If you remember one thing

RNNs read serially into a fixed-size memory: information decays with distance (vanishing gradients, forward dilution) and computation can't parallelize. LSTMs' gates eased the decay but kept the serial bottleneck. Requirements for a successor: direct access to any past position + parallel computation over the whole sequence. Hold those two; they define everything that follows.

Interview questions

Why did transformers replace RNNs/LSTMs? (the canonical question)

Two structural RNN flaws: (1) long-range information must survive sequential compression through a fixed-size hidden state — gradients vanish and memories dilute over distance; (2) computation is inherently serial across time steps, so training can't exploit GPU parallelism over the sequence. Attention gives every position direct, learned access to every other position, computed as parallel matrix multiplies — fixing memory and scalability in one move. The 100× training-cost drop did the rest.

What problem do LSTM gates solve, and what do they NOT solve?

Gates (input/forget/output) learn what to write, erase and expose in a protected cell state, letting gradients survive tens of steps instead of a handful — solving much of the vanishing-gradient problem within moderate ranges. They do NOT remove the fixed-capacity bottleneck (one vector still summarizes everything) and do NOT enable parallel training across time. Patch, not cure.

Walk through the three LSTM gates and the failure each addresses.

All three are learned sigmoid layers outputting values in (0,1), applied element-wise. The forget gate scales the old cell state — addressing "everything gets overwritten": it can hold a memory at ~1.0 for many steps. The input gate scales the newly proposed candidate — addressing "everything gets written": irrelevant tokens can be mostly ignored instead of diluting memory. The output gate filters what the cell state exposes as the hidden state — addressing "memory ≠ what's currently useful": the cell can carry information silently until it's needed. The additive update cₜ = f⊙cₜ₋₁ + i⊙c̃ₜ is what lets gradients flow through the kept portion without repeated matrix squashing.

Why does gradient clipping help exploding but not vanishing gradients?

Clipping rescales the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold — an operation on a number that's too big. An exploding gradient (repeated multiplication by a matrix with gain > 1) arrives huge, gets capped, and training continues with its direction intact. A vanishing gradient arrives as ~10⁻⁸: there's nothing to cap, and naively scaling it up would amplify noise, not signal — the long-range information was destroyed en route, not merely mis-scaled. That asymmetry is why explosion got a training trick while vanishing required architectural fixes: gates and additive paths (LSTM), and ultimately direct connections to the past (attention).

Are RNNs completely dead in 2020s practice?

Mostly for NLP, but not universally: they persist in low-latency streaming settings (tiny per-step cost, O(1) state), some time-series work, and their ideas returned refreshed in modern state-space models (Mamba-family), which offer RNN-like O(n) inference with parallelizable training — the current research challenger to attention's O(n²) (context: M11.2). Mentioning that arc marks you as current rather than dismissive.

An RNN and a transformer both fail on a long document. How do their failure modes differ?

RNN: graceful-but-early degradation — distant information fades continuously; there's no hard limit but effective memory is short. Transformer: sharp boundary — perfect random access within the context window, total amnesia beyond it (plus O(n²) cost growth and some mid-context dilution, M11.2). Summarize-as-you-go vs open-book-with-a-page-limit.
